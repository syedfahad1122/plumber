Water damage risks in Miami

Miami's water damage comes from four directions: storm surge from Biscayne Bay, extreme rainfall that overwhelms street drainage, king tides that push saltwater up through low streets, and the everyday plumbing and AC leaks that happen in any South Florida home or condo tower.

Storm surge: what Hurricane Irma showed

Irma's eye never crossed Miami. It made landfall at Cudjoe Key in the Florida Keys on September 10, 2017, yet its surge still flooded the mainland. The National Weather Service found an average of 3 to 5 feet of inundation along the Biscayne Bay shoreline from Homestead to downtown Miami and Brickell, reaching 1 to 2 blocks inland, with slightly more than 6 feet in isolated spots in Coconut Grove and Brickell (NWS Miami). A storm doesn't need a direct hit to put saltwater in a bayfront building. Surge water is treated as contaminated (Category 3) water, so soaked drywall, insulation and carpet usually have to be removed, not just dried.

Extreme rain: the June 2024 floods

In June 2024 a slow-moving tropical disturbance stalled over South Florida. Parts of northeast Miami-Dade County received more than 22 inches of rain, the National Weather Service issued a flash flood emergency, and both the Governor and Miami-Dade County declared states of emergency. Temporary pumps were set up in Miami Beach and along the Miami River (June 2024 South Florida floods). Rain floods like this can reach homes far from the coast, and water that enters from outside is usually a flood-insurance matter, not a homeowners claim.

King tides and sunny-day flooding

The City of Miami says king tides arrive every year from September through November. Each lasts about three hours and can flood streets near the bay and waterways, and sometimes low inland areas, even on dry days. The City advises staying out of floodwater, washing skin with soap and hot water after contact, and using measures such as sandbags in low-lying areas (City of Miami). NOAA-based forecasts for 2026 point to more king-tide periods on October 7–13, October 24–30, November 6–9 and November 23–26, with impacts starting up to two hours before the predicted peak (WLRN / Florida Storms). If a high tide coincides with heavy rain, drainage backs up faster.

Everyday leaks: AC drains, old cast iron and high-rises

Most calls are not storms. Air conditioners run nearly year-round in Miami, and clogged AC condensate drains are a steady source of ceiling and closet leaks. Many older Miami homes have cast-iron drain lines: a plumber and an attorney interviewed by Fox 4 say homes built before about 1975 commonly used cast iron, which corrodes from both the inside and outside in Florida conditions and can crack and back up. In condo and apartment towers, one failed supply line or water heater can leak through several units below, which is why it matters to shut water off fast and to tell building management right away.

Miami-specific steps after water damage

  1. Stop the water at your own valve first. Close the main shut-off valve for the house or, in a condo, the valve for your unit or the leaking fixture. If you can't find it in a high-rise, call building security or management; they may be able to shut off water to your unit or riser.
  2. Report leaks on the utility side. Miami-Dade Water and Sewer (WASD) is the county's water utility. Its 24-hour emergency call center, (305) 274-9272, takes reports of broken or leaking pipes, hydrants, manholes and meters, sewer backups, and low pressure or loss of service. Its customer-care line, (305) 665-7477, is for billing and accounts (Miami-Dade Water and Sewer contacts). Some cities in the county run their own utilities, so check the name on your water bill.
  3. In a condo, notify the association in writing. Florida condo law splits insurance: the association generally insures the building as originally built, while unit owners must insure items inside their unit that serve only that unit, such as floor, wall and ceiling coverings, electrical fixtures, appliances, water heaters, built-in cabinets, countertops and window treatments (Becker on condo insurance). Read your condo documents and your HO-6 policy, and photograph damage in neighboring units if it came from yours or from theirs.
  4. Document before anything is removed. Take photos and video of every wet room, the water line on walls and the source of the leak. Keep damaged samples (a piece of carpet, baseboard) if you can. See our insurance claim guide for what adjusters usually ask for.
  5. Check which repairs need a permit. The City of Miami's post-storm list says permits are required for wall reconstruction, water heater replacement, water or sewer line repairs, roof repair or replacement, and electrical work such as panel replacement and wiring repairs. Temporary tarping of roofs or walls is exempt, and so are minor cosmetic repairs like painting and carpeting on residential properties when no structural or system changes are made. The list isn't exhaustive, so call the Building Department at (305) 416-1100 if you're unsure (City of Miami Post-Storm Permitting Plan). Other municipalities and unincorporated Miami-Dade have their own building offices.
  6. Know the 50% rule if you're in a flood zone. For buildings in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area, the City of Miami requires a Substantial Damage Determination before it issues a repair permit. If repairs cost 50% or more of the building's pre-storm market value, it is considered substantially damaged and must meet current floodplain rules, which may mean elevating it (City of Miami Post-Storm Permitting Plan).

Rules and phone numbers change, so check the linked official pages for the latest before you act.

Flood zones, evacuation zones and alerts in Miami-Dade

Two different maps matter, and people often confuse them:

  • Storm surge planning zones (evacuation). Miami-Dade uses zones A through E. Zone A is at risk of surge in Category 1 and stronger storms, and Zone E only in Category 5 storms. Look up your address with the county's Know Your Zone map and sign up for Miami-Dade Alerts (Miami-Dade storm surge planning zones). The City of Miami notes that evacuation orders depend on a storm's track and projected surge, not only its category, and lists 311 for shelter and evacuation-route information (City of Miami Hurricane Guide).
  • FEMA flood zones (insurance and building rules). These decide whether a lender requires flood insurance and whether the 50% rule applies. The county's current effective FEMA maps date from September 11, 2009, and FEMA has issued preliminary updates, so check both the current and the preliminary zone. Use the county's flood zone web tool, call its Flood Zone Hot Line at (305) 372-6466 (Miami-Dade flood zone maps), or search your address at the FEMA Flood Map Service Center. Miami-Dade County also reports that its Community Rating System Class 3 rating gives a 35% discount on flood insurance policies issued or renewed on or after April 1, 2024; ask your agent whether it applies to your address.

Standard homeowners and condo policies generally exclude flood, including storm surge and rising water from king tides or heavy rain. That coverage comes from a separate flood policy (NFIP or private). Sudden leaks from inside the home, such as a burst supply line, are handled differently, so read your policy's water-damage section before you need it.

What affects the cost of water damage repair in Miami

No honest source can quote a price before someone sees the damage, but these factors drive the estimate:

  • Type of water. Clean water from a supply line is the simplest. Gray water (washing machine, AC overflow) and black water (sewage, surge, floodwater) need more removal and disinfection.
  • How long it sat. Materials that dry within 24 to 48 hours can often be saved. After that, mold risk rises and more has to be torn out.
  • What got wet. Tile over concrete slab dries differently from carpet, laminate or wood. Wet drywall, insulation and cabinets add demolition and rebuild costs.
  • Building type. High-rise jobs can involve several units, building rules on work hours and elevator use, and coordination with the association's insurer.
  • Permits and flood-zone rules. Plumbing, electrical and wall reconstruction may need permits, and substantial damage in a flood zone brings extra requirements.

Miami water damage questions

Who do I call about a water main break or leaking meter in Miami?

For broken or leaking pipes, hydrants, manholes or meters, sewer backups, or loss of water service, call the Miami-Dade Water and Sewer Department's 24-hour emergency call center at (305) 274-9272. Leaks on your side of the meter, such as inside walls, under the slab or at fixtures, are a job for a licensed plumber. Some cities in the county have their own water utility, so check your bill.

Who pays for water damage in a Miami condo?

Under Florida condo law, the association generally insures the building as originally built, while unit owners must insure items inside their unit that serve only that unit, such as floor, wall and ceiling coverings, appliances, water heaters, cabinets and countertops. Who ultimately pays can also depend on your condo documents and on who caused the leak, so read your declaration and HO-6 policy and notify the association in writing.

Do I need a permit to repair water damage in the City of Miami?

The City of Miami's post-storm list requires permits for wall reconstruction, water heater replacement, water or sewer line repairs, roof repairs and most electrical work. Minor cosmetic work such as painting and carpeting on residential properties is exempt when no structural or system changes are made. Buildings in a FEMA flood zone also need a Substantial Damage Determination after a storm. Call the Building Department at (305) 416-1100 if you're unsure.

Does homeowners insurance cover king tide or storm surge flooding in Miami?

Usually not. Standard homeowners and condo policies generally exclude flood, including storm surge and rising tidal water, which is covered by a separate flood policy (NFIP or private). Sudden leaks from inside the home are handled differently. Check your policy and ask your agent.

How fast can someone get to my home in Miami?

Arrival times depend on the company and on demand, especially after storms. When you call, ask the Miami provider for their expected arrival time before you agree to service.

Do restoration companies in Miami work with insurance?

Many do. Ask the company how they work with your insurer, and check your own policy for what's covered.
